INDS-1135 TikTok & Popular Culture · Deep-dive assignment

Kendrick vs. Drake, Clipped

How TikTok, Reels, Shorts and livestream clips released, explained, judged and finally argued over the biggest rap feud of the 2020s.

Georgey Kurien · Fanshawe College · November 2026

My argument

In older rap feuds, the media reported on the fight after it happened. In 2024, short-form video closed that gap. Clips were where the songs were released, explained, judged and, in the end, used as evidence in court.

I argue that short-form video did more than cover the Drake–Kendrick Lamar feud. It helped decide it, because these platforms reward moments that people can join in on (a chant, a dance, a meme) more than arguments about what is true. The same mechanism let an unproven accusation travel further than any newsroom could check it.

Background

Who, and why it was news

Drake, from Toronto, and Kendrick Lamar, from Compton, California, are two of the biggest names in rap, and both had record deals under Universal Music Group (). That shared label becomes important later. Kendrick is also the Pulitzer Prize–winning writer of DAMN. (), which shaped expectations that his diss tracks would be dense and literary.

Their rivalry had simmered for more than a decade through veiled shots before it exploded in 2024 (; ). The 2024 songs went far beyond bragging. They carried personal accusations: abuse and infidelity aimed at Kendrick, and sexual misconduct involving minors aimed at Drake ().

A note on the accusations. None of the accusations in these songs has been proven. Drake has denied the claims against him (), and the court ruling covered below did not decide whether any were true. It decided they were opinion expressed in a rap battle (). I describe them only as far as needed to explain how they were covered.

Analysis

The four jobs of a clip

Looking across the timeline, I see short-form video doing four different jobs that used to belong to different parts of the media. Two ideas from media studies help explain why.

Theory

Imitation publics

Zulli and Zulli () argue TikTok is designed for imitation: sounds, templates and editing tools invite users to copy and remix the same audio, so people connect by imitating each other.

Theory

Context collapse

Marwick and boyd () describe what happens when one post reaches many audiences at once: the context it was made for disappears.

My idea

Participability

How easy it is for an ordinary person to join in with a 15-second piece of a song. I use it in Job 3 to explain why one song won the feed.

01

The release channel

The songs came out on the feed itself.

Evidence

  • “Taylor Made Freestyle” and “6:16 in LA” appeared on Instagram, not on streaming services ().
  • In the final exchanges, songs went out as YouTube links or straight to social media ().
  • Kendrick replied within about 20 minutes. In Drake’s 2015 feud with Meek Mill, two answer songs came over four days ().

What it means

When a song’s first home is a feed, the event and the coverage arrive at the same moment. There was no gap for a journalist to explain a track before audiences reacted. For most people, the first “report” on each song was someone else’s reaction to it. Traditional outlets still mattered, but they were covering a story that clips had already framed.

02

The newsroom

Reaction creators broke and explained the story.

Evidence

  • A fifth of U.S. adults regularly get news on TikTok, including 39% of adults under 30 ().
  • TikTok news consumers are about as likely to get news there from influencers or celebrities as from journalists ().
  • Streamers on Twitch, YouTube and Kick competed to react first, and critic Anthony Fantano posted at least six live reaction videos in about two weeks ().
  • Decoding lyrics and building theories became part of the fun for fans ().

What it means

Reaction creators did a newsroom’s job: break the story, explain it and add background. Their incentives were different, though. A reporter is rewarded for being accurate. A reaction creator is rewarded for being first, funny and visibly emotional. Viewers trusted the reaction, not the sourcing. That worked well for explaining wordplay and badly for weighing serious accusations.

03

The jury

Memes, streams and trends became the scoreboard.

Evidence

  • Memes worked as badges that showed which side you were on ().
  • One viral post summed up the mood: “every stream is a vote” ().
  • “Not Like Us” made TikTok’s U.S. Songs of the Summer list () and was YouTube’s top trending song of 2024 ().
  • “BBL Drizzy” turned the feud into a contest and dance trend anyone could enter (; ). See the open-verse case study.

What it means: participability

On an imitation platform, the song people can copy spreads furthest. Drake’s strongest songs needed context: you had to know the people and rumours he was referencing to feel the hit. “Not Like Us” could be performed by a kid in a car. I scored each track on four traits to test the idea.

Chantable hookDance-friendly beatShort quotable lineWorks without context

My own qualitative rating, 1 to 5 per trait (maximum 20). The two highest scores, “Not Like Us” and “BBL Drizzy,” are the two pieces of the feud that clearly broke out on TikTok and beyond (; ). The scores are subjective and Kendrick’s writing still matters, but the pattern suggests the feud was judged partly on which side gave the audience something to do.

04

The evidence

The clips ended up in court.

Evidence

  • Drake’s first petition argued the song’s popularity was manufactured with bots and paid promotion ().
  • His lawsuit named reaction videos as proof that UMG let creators repost the song freely () and cited TikTok and YouTube posts as part of the harm ().
  • The judge rejected the bot and payola claims and criticized their reliance on online comments and unverified reports ().
  • She ruled that a reasonable listener would hear the lyrics as part of a heated rap battle ().

What it means

Here the loop closes. Both sides used the internet as evidence. Drake pointed to its scale to argue harm and manipulation. The court treated the online chatter as unreliable, and treated the very public battle as the reason no reasonable listener would take a diss lyric as fact.

The irony is that the same virality that spread the accusation also made its context famous. Drake’s appeal argues the judge was wrong to read the song only inside the whole battle (), and points to huge audiences like the Super Bowl (). That is the context-collapse problem, now in front of an appeals court.

Case study

Everyone gets a diss: the “BBL Drizzy” open verse

For two weeks the feud was a fight between two famous rappers. On May 5, 2024, a producer handed the microphone to everyone else.

  1. Apr 2024A nickname

    Rick Ross mocks Drake online as “BBL Drizzy,” a joke about rumoured cosmetic surgery ().

  2. Apr 14An AI joke song

    Comedian King Willonius writes a parody and uses the AI tool Udio to turn it into a fake 1970s soul record (; ).

  3. May 3The insult

    On “Family Matters,” Drake takes a shot at producer Metro Boomin ().

  4. May 5The open verse

    Metro samples the AI song, posts the beat free on SoundCloud and promises a free beat to whoever raps the best verse over it (; ).

  5. May 6The prize

    He raises it to $10,000 plus a beat, with a beat for the runner-up ().

  6. May 5–12The flood

    Entries pour in on TikTok, Instagram, X and YouTube. The beat passes 3.3 million SoundCloud plays in a week and tops the site’s New and Hot chart (; ).

  7. May 24The target joins in

    Drake raps over the same beat on Sexyy Red’s “U My Everything,” turning the joke back on itself ().

  8. Jun 2024The lawsuit

    Major labels sue Udio, the AI company whose tool made the original song (). See the legal timeline.

Who joined in

Aspiring rappers

Thousands of aspiring artists recorded verses aimed at Drake, and submissions arrived from around the world (; ). Four days in, XXL was already publishing a list of 20 standout entries (). Yuno Miles’s freestyle trended on YouTube Music and passed half a million views in a day ().

Genre-benders

People rebuilt the beat as merengue, Jersey club, a stadium-rock guitar solo, a saxophone feature, a Bollywood track and a tour of 1990s regional rap styles (; ; ).

Brands and public figures

The Duolingo English Test’s owl mascot joined the trend (; ), and celebrity plastic surgeon “Dr. Miami” posted a TikTok of the beat playing in his office ().

People who don’t rap

The sound became a TikTok dance trend (), and reaction creators made series out of reviewing other people’s entries, including ones performed in other languages ().

How the press covered it

Most of the coverage was built out of fan posts. XXL ran a best-entries list, Complex and HotNewHipHop ran remix roundups, and Gizmodo made a slideshow of the strangest versions (; ; ; ). The Guardian went further and called “BBL Drizzy” the real winner of the feud (). Billboard treated it as a business story: possibly the first major hip-hop beat built on an AI-generated sample, and a sign of where sampling is heading (). In Canada, one columnist counted Metro Boomin among the feud’s big winners because of how many independent rappers, singers and reggaeton artists were recording over his beat ().

What it means: the audience writes the diss

This is participability at its most extreme. Kendrick’s songs asked people to listen and pick a side. Metro’s beat asked them to make something. Zulli and Zulli (2022) describe TikTok users as an imitation public that connects by copying shared sounds and templates (), and here the template was literally a blank diss track. For about a week, the loudest voice against Drake wasn’t Kendrick but thousands of strangers, and the news coverage was mostly lists of their videos.

My take

Two things bother me about this moment. First, it was one-sided: the prize rewarded mocking Drake, so “everyone can join” really meant everyone joining in against one person, which connects to the harassment described in The cost. Second, it ran on an AI-made song. That was funny until people started asking who owns a voice or a sound, the same question behind the Udio lawsuit.

I could not find a report that Metro Boomin ever publicly named a contest winner. Coverage at the time noted there was no deadline (). The fan-made videos themselves are in Creator watch.

Case study

The one who walked away: J. Cole

Cole helped start the feud, fired one shot, then did something almost nobody in rap does: he took it back. How people judged that choice changed as the feud got uglier.

  1. Oct 2023The boast

    On Drake’s “First Person Shooter,” Cole names himself, Drake and Kendrick as rap’s big three (). See the song.

  2. Mar 22, 2024Kendrick’s answer

    On “Like That,” Kendrick rejects the big three, aiming at both Drake and Cole (; ).

  3. Apr 5The shot

    Cole’s surprise project Might Delete Later closes with “7 Minute Drill,” his reply to Kendrick (; ).

  4. Apr 7The apology

    Headlining his own Dreamville Festival, Cole tells the crowd the song didn’t feel right, apologizes to Kendrick and says he’ll pull it. Most people see it through attendees’ phone videos (; ). The next day it was still No. 2 on Apple Music’s streaming chart ().

  5. Apr 12The deletion

    “7 Minute Drill” can no longer be played on Apple Music, Spotify or YouTube (; ).

  6. May 4The memes

    The weekend Drake and Kendrick trade their harshest songs, “J. Cole living his best life” memes go viral (; ).

  7. May 8The beach

    A fan’s TikTok of meeting Cole at the beach is treated as proof the memes were true (; ).

  8. Oct 9His explanation

    On “Port Antonio,” released straight to Instagram and YouTube, Cole says he stepped away rather than lose a friendship or fuel a fight that could turn violent for money, and he sends love to Drake (; ).

  9. Mar 2026Looking back

    In an Apple Music interview, Cole says he loves both artists and that shortly before his set he realized the diss didn’t reflect how he felt about Kendrick ().

Fact check

Did Kendrick tell him to step out?

Not on the public record. Kendrick never answered “7 Minute Drill” before Cole apologized, and afterwards he left Cole alone and focused on Drake (). The closest thing to an instruction is a second-hand claim, spread on social media and never confirmed by either rapper, that Kendrick’s TDE labelmate ScHoolboy Q warned Cole to back off (). Fans also joked that someone must have told Cole to get out of the way (). Cole’s own explanations point to his conscience and his friendships, not to an order (; ). So “Kendrick told him to” is how many fans read the story, not something that has been confirmed.

How the verdict flipped

  1. April 7–8“He folded”

    Many people online and in hip-hop tore Cole down for backing out (). One columnist later admitted he had thought Cole looked soft ().

  2. May 3–7“He escaped”

    Once the accusations turned serious, fans started calling his exit the smartest move in the feud, and the memes recast him as the only one at peace (; ; ).

  3. June 19“He dodged a bullet”

    During Kendrick’s Pop Out concert, fans joked about how relieved Cole must be ().

  4. Oct 2024 →“Still debated”

    Cole explained himself on “Port Antonio,” but some rappers, like Oschino, still said he shouldn’t have backed out (; ). The memes kept celebrating his peace ().

What it means: the bystander becomes a character

Short-form video turned the person who left the feud into one of its biggest characters. Cole didn’t have to do anything. The “living his best life” format took every new accusation between Drake and Kendrick and turned it into a joke about Cole’s peace (). This is the jury from Job 03 working in real time: the same decision was re-scored every time the feud got worse.

My take

Cole’s exit shows that the internet judges a choice by how the story ends, not by whether it was right when it was made. In April, apologizing looked like losing. By May, after the worst accusations came out, the same choice looked like the smartest move of the feud. Nothing about Cole’s decision changed in between. What changed was what everyone else was watching. It also says something that so many people believe Kendrick told him to leave, even though that has never been confirmed. The story just feels better with a showdown in it.

Critical analysis

The cost of a clip-shaped story

A serious accusation became a sing-along

The “A minor” line rests on an unproven accusation, yet a Super Bowl stadium sang it (). Writers at the Daily Beast argued the meme-heavy reaction turned allegations about real people into entertainment (). A battle insult reached audiences who knew it only as a hook.

Rumours outran corrections

The “secret daughter” claim spread as a meme before Drake replied (). His claim that the story was planted () never travelled as far. Short-form rewards the first version of a story, not the corrected one.

Online heat, offline harm

Drake’s lawsuit says the song led to attempted break-ins, a flyer calling him a predator and pulling his child from school (). These are claims in a complaint, and police did not link the May 2024 shooting to the feud at the time (). They still show that the people inside a “content” story are real.

AI entered the fight

Drake’s AI Tupac verse came down after the estate objected (). The AI-made “BBL Drizzy” became a hit sample (), and the company behind the tool was later sued by major labels (). The feud became an early test of who owns a voice.

Counterpoint

Did short-form really decide it?

There are three good reasons to be careful with my argument.

Craft still mattered.NPR’s critic judged that Drake treated the feud like a game Kendrick wasn’t playing (). Good writing gave the clips something to spread.
Institutions sealed it.The Grammys () and the Super Bowl stage () are old-media gatekeepers. They amplified the verdict, and they reached people who never open TikTok.
The market didn’t fully agree.In May 2026, Drake’s Iceman opened at No. 1 and his three new albums took the top three spots (). Losing the argument did not mean losing the audience.

My conclusion: short-form video decided the story the public told about the feud, institutions confirmed that story, and Drake’s sales show that a feed’s verdict and an audience’s habits are different things.

Conclusion

What this teaches about celebrity scandals

The Drake–Kendrick feud shows how much short-form video has changed what “media coverage” means. Clips did four jobs at once: they released the songs, explained them, judged them and became evidence in court. The song built for participation won the public verdict, and the same machinery spread an unproven accusation further than any correction could follow.

Three lessons I take from this for following any scandal:

  1. Ask where you first saw the story. If it was a reaction clip, you got someone’s feelings before you got the facts.
  2. Participation is not verification. Millions of people chanting a line doesn’t make it true. A federal judge said the same thing about this song ().
  3. The most participable version wins. Whatever is easiest to copy spreads furthest, whether or not it is accurate.

A view from Ontario

From here, this was also a local story: a Toronto star, a Toronto house on a cover image, a shooting covered by local news, and CBC writing explainers for people who never follow rap (). Short-form video made a Compton–Toronto rivalry feel like something everyone in my feed had an opinion on, whether or not they had heard the songs.

    Analysis

    The exit point

    I mark April 24, 2024 as the moment the feud left the music scene. That is when a lawyer for Tupac Shakur’s estate told Drake to take down a song built on an AI copy of Tupac’s voice (). Until then, the only people deciding anything were artists and fans. After it, outside institutions (estates, police, labels, courts) kept getting pulled in.

    Two weeks later the spillover turned physical. A security guard was shot outside Drake’s Toronto home, though police did not connect it to the feud at the time (). Drake’s lawsuit later grouped that night with attempted break-ins, a flyer near his home and pulling his son from school as harms he blames on the song (). Those are claims in a complaint, not proven facts, but they show how quickly an online pile-on can become a safety problem for real people.

    The court phase also kept looping back to the internet. Drake tried to prove the song’s popularity was manufactured, and the judge found those claims rested on online comments and unverified reports (). The same social media that spread the song could not prove how it spread.
